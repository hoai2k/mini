Different ways of seeing.

A microscope view, a physically motivated immersive rendering and an electron-image treatment can each look realistic while making different claims.

Field notes / 30 October 2026 · Biology, visual references & possibilities for simulation

Three meanings of realism

Instrument realism reproduces how a microscope forms an image. Biological realism preserves anatomy, behavior and scale. Hypothetical scene realism imagines light interacting with that world from an immersive viewpoint. These can support each other, but none automatically guarantees the others.

Optics constrain what can be seen.

Higher magnification does not by itself resolve smaller structures. Numerical aperture, wavelength, acquisition and contrast determine what information survives. Depth of field can be very shallow at high NA; an entire thick animal and the background cannot all be equally sharp in one honest optical section. [S17] · [S18]

DIC makes transparent bodies look sculpted by converting differences in optical path to contrast. Its apparent shadows are an imaging effect, not sunlight falling across tiny hills. Do not derive true height from that relief alone. [S19]

A game-like view might deliberately widen focus or exaggerate a boundary for readability. Label that compromise. Use selective emphasis and consistent material cues before resorting to a field full of hard outlines.

Electron microscopes do not supply natural lighting.

Conventional SEM uses an electron beam and detected signals, not a miniature sun. Many biological samples are fixed, dehydrated and coated to remain stable and conductive in vacuum. Monochrome topography-like contrast is compelling, but it does not show the color or normal wet transparency of a living organism. [S20]

Cryo-SEM can preserve frozen hydrated material; environmental SEM is another exception to conventional dry preparation. These methods have their own conditions and artifacts, and do not establish that a normal SEM scene could host free swimming life. TEM commonly images thin prepared sections; the cited Paramecium dataset specifies approximately 75 nm resin sections. [S21] · [S22]

Concept boards, side by side.

How to use the paintings responsibly.

All four boards are generated visual hypotheses, saved locally as WebP. No web reference image was supplied as an edit target; the text prompts describe the scenes independently. The saved prompt set records the brief and tool.

Known conceptual departures: cilia and flagella are enhanced; transparent bodies have more contrast than many live brightfield specimens; the water-film board dramatizes menisci and may imply incorrect film thickness; perspective panels are imagined rather than reconstructions. The mite’s first panel mixes optical cues. Generated legs, claws, organs and shell pores must be checked against the real references before modeling; the tardigrade must have exactly four leg pairs.

The panels are not calibrated images, taxonomic identifications or evidence of co-occurrence. Use them to compare mood, spatial readability and material treatment. Use reference micrographs and living footage to establish morphology, motion and measurements.

2D and 3D are spatial choices, not styles.

A 2D simulation could use real microscope-like contrast, a painted environment, or an image rendered from 3D geometry. A 3D simulation could still present a fixed microscope camera. Separate dimensionality of movement, geometry used for rendering, camera freedom and the optical treatment.

Shallow layers may suit water films; full 3D may be valuable around filaments or in pore spaces. A readable fixed view can communicate biology more clearly than unrestricted camera movement. These are comparison points, not a selected presentation for a game.
