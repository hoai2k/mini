Use the material at its own scale.

Water is geometry as well as motion.

Surface, film, volume

A partly wet leaf offers exposed surface routes and submerged routes. A film has a thickness, a connected footprint and an interface; those should not collapse into one generic “wetness” value.

In an open pocket, swimmers can move above attached organisms. In a thin film, nearby walls and the air–water boundary alter motion. In a coverslipped sample, the glass is another real boundary. Representing one thin plane is a useful approximation only when the chosen habitat justifies it.

Environmental change

Evaporation can narrow connections and change solute concentrations; rewetting can reconnect patches and redistribute particles. These are plausible dynamics to investigate rather than specified timed hazards.

The position of an air–water interface is different from a current or a diffuse chemical field. A believable visual treatment should distinguish those phenomena.

The fluid-mechanics review supports the low-Reynolds-number interpretation. A surface crawler additionally needs adhesion and deformation; swimming equations alone do not model amoeboid movement. [S15]

Chemistry is usually invisible.

Biofilms can develop internal resource gradients because metabolism and transport interact. An oxygen-poor region should not automatically be rendered as black poison fog. A scientific overlay could use isolines, arrows or a separate color channel, with an explicit label distinguishing data from optical appearance. [S16]

To assess a future scene, ask what organisms actually occur together, the water chemistry and temperature, the physical size of each gap, and how the image was acquired.
