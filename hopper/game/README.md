# Hopper the Grasshopper

A complete browser adventure in three episodes, built around Xbox controllers and fullscreen television/monitor play. The presentation uses generated, hand-painted 1970s anime artwork: a canonical Hopper model, twelve registered animation cels, eighteen shadow species, three bosses, nine environments, separate terrain/decor sprites, and an eight-cel explosion atlas.

## Two editions

The published page at https://hoai2k.github.io/mini/hopper/ opens the 2D game with a single Play button. `?render=3d` opens the 3D edition and shows both Play 2D and Play 3D on the title screen; picking one rebuilds the engine and the canvas it draws into without reloading the page. The 3D edition is powered by three.js and built around high-speed movement, mantling, wall-kicks, soaring and gliding. Both editions share the title screen, play-select, menus, settings, and audio, but maintain separate save files: the 3D edition uses localStorage keys prefixed `hopper3d.*`, while the 2D edition uses `hopper.*`. The 3D code lives in `src/game3d/` with world colliders loaded from stand-ins (world.ts), movement and jumping (controller.ts), camera control and framing (camera.ts), combat (combat3d.ts), the districts (district.ts), the route each one follows (route.ts), the trail dressed along it (trail.ts) with the solid things that flank it (trailprops.ts), the world either side of it (scenery.ts), three.js rendering (scene.ts), and the main loop (engine3d.ts).

Beyond the trail's own shoulder, `scenery.ts` generates the rest of the country from the district: clumps of the region's tall things fill the middle distance out to 430 m (instanced, in trail.ts), and real structures — farmhouses and silos, towers and roof decks, crag columns and ledges — stand 150–430 m off the path with their own colliders, so Hopper can go and climb them. It also gives every span somewhere to land: a bridge or an elevated rail is placed with an abutment under each end of its deck (a rock pier, a city block) and one more span beyond that, so a crossing comes from somewhere and goes somewhere instead of starting and stopping in mid-air. The Thunderhead viaduct now spans the storm gorge at rim height, on piers, with the trail running along the floor beneath it. Generated scenery keeps 120 m off the trail and never buries a totem, signal, capsule or pad.

Districts are **walked through**, not handed over. The next district stands on the horizon the whole way — each region's landmark is the place it leads to, painted in that place's own colours — and from a few hundred metres out it is the real thing: the next district's own terrain and structures, built and drawn standing on this district's exit. The trail ends at a threshold, a lit arch across the way on with the destination framed through it, and crossing it is one more stride. There is no pause, no held frame and no dissolve: the world under Hopper is rebuilt inside the step he crosses on, and because the next district's start sits exactly on this one's exit, he keeps the offset he crossed at — the ground under his feet does not move. He carries his heading measured from the trail (so a run continues as a run, angled the way it was), his speed, and his flight if he jumped through; the camera keeps its manual turn and pitch and comes through facing the way on.

Crossing rebuilds a district's world and its shadows in one step, so that rebuild had to be worth doing in one step: finding somewhere for a stronghold's host to stand used to scan every collider in the district and probe the real ground under thousands of them, which cost Thunderhead Range — 56,572 baked boxes — nearly two seconds of its build. It now takes the boxes from the spatial grid and refuses on a box's own claimed top before paying for a ground probe, which is provably the same choice of perch for a tenth of the work; the district builds in about 350 ms, and the other eight in 60–110 ms. That is a brief hitch at the seam rather than a transition, and it makes every checkpoint restart and first load quicker too.

The one thing that could interrupt that is art that has not arrived, so it is fetched before it is needed: from 700 m out — about ten seconds at a run, three at a spring — the approach quietly loads the next district's paintings, and by the time the threshold is reached they are in hand. A seam somehow reached cold still raises the loading hold rather than dropping Hopper into a half-painted district, but in play it is not seen. Nothing else about the crossing changed: each district is still its own world, with its own checkpoints, its own shadows and its own save slot, and the commander still bars the last threshold of a mission until it is down.

A **lockdown gate** is a barrier you can see: while a gate or a boss arena is sealed, a ribbed violet wall of light stands at exactly the radius Hopper is held inside (the clamp is a cylinder, so the wall is one too), it flares where he pushes against it, and the HUD keeps saying what would open it — the number of shadows still alive inside, or that the commander holds the gate. Refusing the district exit names the same count and points back to the dome.

Each 3D district is a **trail**: a smooth route from the start totem through every checkpoint to the exit, authored as waypoints in `district.ts` so it winds between the structures, climbs the terraces and plateaus, drops through the valleys and keeps going forward. The route is one idea three things share. The ground is painted with a worn path along it and dressed by `trail.ts` with a ribbon the width of Hopper's stance, edge stones, lit waymarkers every 55 m, and tall side props standing 20–95 m back (trees and haystacks in the fields, pylons and lamps in the city, rock spires and cairns in the mountains) so the size of a 14 m grasshopper and the distance ahead read. Those props are solid: `trailprops.ts` decides where each one stands and how much mass it carries, the picture draws them and the world puts a collider on the same trunk, so a tree stops Hopper instead of letting him through. A trunk or a shaft is too narrow a face to climb or mantle onto; a pylon, a rock spire and a boulder are not. **Climbing never interrupts a run, and neither does a low obstacle.** There are three bands. Anything under 5 m — a kerb, rubble, a low wall — is not an obstacle at all: it is not a wall to resolve against, so it never stops a run and never blocks a spring launched flat across it, and the ground under him simply steps up onto its top. From 5 m up to 12 m is a **scramble**: running into a lip that high hauls him up onto it, so a terrace riser or a low roof is something he climbs over in stride rather than a dead stop with the stick still pressed into it — which is what "stuck on its side" felt like. He only scrambles where there is somewhere to stand: he has to come down on the lip itself, and a body's width further in must not fall away under him (a step up there is fine, since that is what the next riser of a terrace looks like). Above 12 m he is stopped, as by anything solid. Taking hold of a wall and going up it is something he has to jump into: it needs his feet off the ground, the stick pushed at the face, a face that carries on above his own standing height (14 m — anything shorter he can clear), and a face wide enough to stand on (5 m of half-width, so a mast or a trunk is not a wall). Running at a wall stops him at it; jumping at one climbs it. The ground he stands on is the ground that is drawn: the height field is read across the terrain mesh's own triangles rather than the smooth function they sample, which used to leave the collision surface up to 34 m from the picture in the worst places and his feet under the grass in many more. The follow camera faces along the route's tangent 80 m ahead of Hopper's place on it, never at a point, so it only ever pans as the trail bends (capped at 0.9 rad/s) and never faces back toward the start; Hopper can turn round and run toward the camera and the view holds. Checkpoints restart Hopper facing along the trail. There are **two jumps**. **A is the ordinary one**: it fires the instant the button goes down and holding it jumps higher — let go on the way up and the rise stops there, so a tap reaches 26 m and a full hold 96 m, with everything between a matter of how long it is held. Keep holding past the top and the wings take over as they always did, into the hover and then the glide. **LB is the spring**, the super jump: held on the ground it stops Hopper where he stands and winds up, and letting go launches it, so that button is a wind rather than an impulse. For the first 0.3 s of the hold nothing is given up at all — a mis-tap never costs a stride — and a release inside that window is the smallest spring, taken at full stride. Hold past it and he stops where he stands and the wind begins, reaching full 1.5 s after the press. The wind sets one launch speed and **the stick sets the angle it leaves at**: pushed fully forward that is 15° above the ground, a flat dart that reads as a lunge rather than a jump; neutral it is 45°; pulled fully back it is straight up. A half-pushed stick lands halfway between, and a stick pushed sideways aims sideways at 45°. Pulling back never launches Hopper backwards — the backward part of the stick only stands the launch up, and whatever forward or sideways part is left sets the direction. A tap cannot reach the flattest angle (fully forward at no wind is 25°), so the lunge has to be earned, and a spring never leaves slower along its own line than Hopper was already travelling, so a tap taken at a sprint keeps the sprint. At 68 m/s running and gravity 120 m/s² (1.4× on the way down) that gives a 4 m apex over 45 m of ground for a forward tap, 14 m over 152 m for a 0.75 s hold, 16 m over 262 m for a full one at 325 m/s, 120 m over 550 m for a full wind held neutral, and a 240 m climb straight up for a full wind pulled back — the highest jump in the game. The crouch shows all of this before it happens: Hopper sinks deeper the longer the spring winds and tips back toward wherever it is aimed, and at full wind he throbs against the stop inside a ring of light while the pad knocks once. A dive falls at up to 190 m/s, and airborne steering turns the arc without ever slowing it — before, a jump taken at a sprint was dragged back to running pace in mid-air, which is what made every leap feel vertical. Pushing the stick back in the air still brakes hard, which is the only air brake there is: there is no held guard in this edition either, and B (L on the keyboard) is a second kick button, so a shot coming in is met by turning and sweeping rather than by raising anything. The commander perches within a leap of its arena floor (96 m, not 215) and is a first-class target: the eye lasers auto-aim at it, the lock-on can hold it, and while it is awake the camera lifts its look toward it and stands up to 22 m further back so it stays in frame. The HUD shows its name, phase, health and current tell; every other shadow that is locked or being shot at gets a smaller name-and-health readout under the compass, so the damage landing is always visible. Every shadow is scaled to Hopper's size or beyond (a hound 14 m at the shoulder, a spitter 16 m, a tortoise 15 m, a ray 31 m across, a condor 42 m, a leech 35 m long), with colliders scaled to match, so the enemies of a giant are worth fighting.

## Play

Play the published game at **https://hoai2k.github.io/mini/hopper/**. `pnpm build:pages` produces the static GitHub Pages edition; pushes to `main` deploy it automatically.

Run `pnpm install`, then `pnpm dev`. Open the displayed local address. The title screen carries a single action: Press Start, Enter, or any controller button opens the play-select screen, requests fullscreen and starts the music where autoplay was refused. Play-select lists Continue (once there is a save) and the unlocked episodes, with the controller diagram alongside. Browsers that decline a fullscreen or audio request from controller polling can be activated with the on-screen fullscreen and sound buttons.

The three gaits are told apart by what the body does, not just the feet: the gallop bounds, one long beat a stride with the nose rising as the hind legs drive, while the walk carries the body level and lets six legs do the work - a walk that bobbed and rolled with the stride read as a waddle rather than an insect. Spring pads launch at 1.4× jump speed (hold A to float higher). Takeoff strikes behind Hopper. Descending onto vulnerable monsters produces a stomp rebound. In 3D that rebound is a kick downward rather than a trampoline: it reaches 28 m (42 off a hover or a glide), half what it used to, and gravity pulls 1.9× harder while he is rising off the back, so he leaves fast and is over the top in about half the time. What says the stomp landed is the snap, not the height. Airborne steering changes the trajectory without turning Hopper; facing can change once grounded. The compact flight cel remains active until descending within 80 world units of a platform or monster. Defence is the spin kick, in both editions. It turns all the way round: in 2D it damages anything within 165 units on either side of Hopper and overhead, in 3D anything within 16 m of his body and 6 m above or below it, drawing a bright arc out to that reach as it sweeps, and timed as a blow arrives, from any direction, it parries. A new kick can start before the last one has finished, so a volley of shots is turned one after another. A parry staggers a shadow wide open and sends a shot back at its shooter as a kick. Hazards cannot be parried. There is no held guard: B and L are a second kick button. Eye lasers lock onto the nearest shadow ahead within a wide cone that reaches well below Hopper, so a shelf below can be cleared from above; solid terrain and unbroken cages stop the beam where it meets them. A closed shell (the crag tortoise, and the second episode's caster, crab and wasp) turns the beam aside instead of taking it: the tortoise throws it off at a steep angle where it dies out along its line, while the second episode's shells mirror it back whole, a little off Hopper's own line, so a beam can come back and hit him. A kick or a turned-back shot opens any shell for a moment. Only a few hardened shadows in the third episode refuse a stomp as well as the lasers while closed. A jump pressed while touching a solid face (or within 0.12 s of leaving it) is a wall kick: Hopper launches away from the wall at 92% jump speed and 78% run speed, with steering committed for the first 0.16 s. The right stick pushes the camera up to 520 units ahead or behind and 340 up or down while widening the view by up to 0.22 zoom; it eases out toward the stick and eases back when released, layered over the tracking camera so nothing about the ground-anchored framing changes. A hit that lands knocks Hopper back a real distance with a short stun, so edges are the true cost of a mistake. Falling just short of a ledge while pressing toward it makes Hopper catch the lip and haul up (solid faces from up to 85 units below; thin shelves only as the feet pass the edge). A missed jump is not a death: below every route lies a dark floor, a gentle slope that climbs roughly with the shelves but drifts further under them, and Hopper slides backwards on it until he reaches a launch pad, one every 1,000 units. On a pad he can only jump, and that jump fires him straight up to just above the nearest shelf overhead, however far up it is, and back into play.

The camera prioritizes stable terrain. Ordinary jumps cause no vertical tracking. Very high leaps and deep drops receive screen-edge protection. Horizontal look-ahead reverses after sustained travel (at least 0.38 seconds and 150 units), so short aiming inputs do not whip the view around. The primary logical composition is 1600×900; fullscreen HUD and menu typography adapt to standard monitor/TV sizes.

All connected browser-recognized gamepads control the same character and menus. Buttons combine across controllers; movement and look axes add together, capped at normal speed, with equal opposite directions cancelling. Keyboard controls remain available. Disconnecting one controller does not interrupt play while another remains connected.

## Campaign

1. **Earthbound Thunder:** Sunseed Fields → Crownline City → Thunderhead Range. Agricultural terraces, a metropolis with tall roof climbs, then storm gorges and summits. Boss: Night Rook.
2. **The Iron Migration:** Cinder Foundries → Tempest Docks → Skyhook Works. Casting lines, crane forests, descent shafts and launch scaffolds. Boss: Smelter Leviathan.
3. **Beyond the Black Sun:** Vermilion Basin (1.35g) → Cobalt Drift (0.55g) → Violet Inversion (0.85g, optional reversed-gravity galleries). Boss: the Eclipse Regent.

Each mission has twelve named chapters, roughly 52–61k world units (three fifths of what the boards were, and a little over half of what they were before that: a shelf is now a landing, the beat it carries and a takeoff, and nothing is there only to be walked across), 55–74 authored enemy spawns (14 to 25 per region, about a quarter ambushes, up to 40% agile, and up to a third arriving as later waves), 25 checkpoints, nine optional signal collectibles, 6–7 high roads over fights, 2–3 low roads under them, a catch floor with a spring pad at every chapter's crossing, 3 swinging main-route shelves, 3 belts that run against Hopper, 3 crosswind lanes, 3 split crossings with an island, 3 signal cages, in the third episode one required inverted crossing, and a 3,400-unit boss enclosure sealed by lockdown walls while the boss lives. Every board is sequenced rather than repeated. Every region builds four chapters rather than five: the fourth was a second turn of the same mixed rhythm between the opener, the glide chapter with its low road and cage, and the summit with its swinging shelf, belt and island, so it is gone and the three that carry something keep their places. A chapter is one of three shapes: `leaps` is jumping alone, five shelves (a climb or two more where the region's gravity would otherwise ask too much of one jump) that share the chapter's whole elevation change between them, with nothing to fight and an unguarded crossing; `skirmish` is fighting alone on level ground, two fights and a finish with one breath between them; `mixed` is the lesson, one encounter, the rest checkpoint, the crossing and the finish, plus the one travel beat that tells it from the others - a run, a drop, a press or a climb. A second fight on a second shelf of the same shape was the same encounter twice and the spare run and climb between them were ground to cross, so neither survives. A chapter grows a shelf where its own climb would otherwise ask one jump for more rise than it reaches, and every shelf's width scales with the region's gravity, because a shelf sized as a landing at 1 g is flown over at 0.55. Board one runs Sunseed Fields as leaps, skirmish, leaps, mixed, Crownline City as mixed, leaps, mixed, mixed and Thunderhead Range as leaps, skirmish, mixed, mixed; later boards open every region on a leaps chapter. Each of a region's chapters also has a movement signature that changes what its jumps ask for: the lesson chapter is even; the ladder takes its climb in tall chunks between flat landings over short gaps; the glide runs long gaps from height that want a hold and a release to brake, opens with a low road under its fight, and ends with a spring-pad crossing under a crosswind; the sprint keeps gaps tight and shelves near-flat for low fast hops; the summit mixes them, swings one shelf, runs one fight on a belt against Hopper, and splits its crossing with an island a shadow surfaces on. The three signal shelves per region each reward a verb: one lip sits just past a full jump so only the ledge catch reaches it, one is caged with its own shooter, and the cage comes apart under ordinary fire or a spin kick rather than waiting on a parried shot, one perches above the high road. Encounter clusters and recovery stretches vary between chapters. Checkpoints save locally, restore partial armor on arrival and restart nearby after defeat. Completing an episode unlocks the next. Most shadows are simple, but from the second chapter a growing share are agile: hounds pounce, ground shooters vault over Hopper and fire from behind, shooting flyers overfly to the blind side, divers come straight back for a second pass, and shells hop. Encounters grow by waves that leap in as the first wave falls (up to five on a shelf by the last episode), and every chapter crossing is contested by a flyer over the gap or a shooter on the far landing, so the hardest moments are jumps fought through; the level audit verifies that no transition is both a hard jump and a heavy fight. Crossing the mouth of a boss arena wakes the boss and drops lockdown walls at both ends until it dies; inside are pillars to wall-kick, staggered shelves, a swinging shelf and spring pads. Bosses have three health phases, readable windups, attack patterns and exposed-core recovery windows. The Night Rook, the first board's commander, is a fight about turning its own fire back: it crosses its arena from one side to the other rather than hanging over the middle, its heavy shots are thrown at Hopper instead of rolled along the floor, and its wings come up on a cycle - while they are up its ribcage is lit in cold light and a beam that meets it comes straight back, so a shot parried into that chest is worth several times what a laser, a kick or a stomp lands. A stagger drops the guard. Shots are aimed at a boss's chest rather than the middle of its compact combat box, since the painted body stands far higher than the box does. See `qa/COMBAT_AUDIT.md`.

The Smelter Leviathan and Eclipse Regent also patrol from side to side, aim their heavy shots at Hopper, cycle a visible laser-reflecting guard, and take their heaviest damage from reflected fire. The Leviathan crosses on the floor while the Regent passes overhead.

Music is provided by the user: **Hopper the Grasshopper** accompanies the title and menus and carries on, uninterrupted, into episode one; **Grass March 1** loops through episode two, and **Dark Moon** through the alien world of episode three. Track positions are preserved when changing between menus, episodes and play, and when the tab is hidden or unfocused: leaving the tab pauses the music and suspends the effect audio, and returning resumes the same track at the same position. Music and effects have separate volume controls, and a sound button sits with the other icon buttons on the play-select screen and in the HUD. The theme starts on its own where the browser permits autoplay; where it is refused the sound button shows as off until it is pressed.

## Implementation and verification

`src/game/engine.ts` owns the fixed 120 Hz simulation, camera and right-stick look, parry, knockback, ledge catch, wall kick, saves and combat integration. `levels.ts` defines the routes, `combat.ts` defines enemies/bosses, `renderer.ts` composites the artwork and effects, and keeps a pixel budget: the 2D picture is fill-rate bound - every frame repaints the whole screen several times over - so the backing store is sized to what the machine is actually keeping up with rather than to `devicePixelRatio`, never below three fifths of the screen's own grid (`qa/tests/render-budget.mjs`) (including the generated upgrade pack under `public/assets/upgrades/`: spring pads, boss lockdown gates, signal cages, wind lanes, low-road corridors, airborne enemy cels, arena pillars and the emergence burst), `hopper-animation.ts` selects registered cels and eye origins, and `input.ts` / `audio.ts` handle devices and sound. React owns the title, HUD, accessible menus and settings. The 3D edition uses `src/game3d/engine3d.ts`, `controller.ts`, `camera.ts`, `combat3d.ts`, `district.ts`, `world.ts`, and `scene.ts` for movement, camera control, combat, world loading, and three.js rendering; `qa/tests/engine3d.mjs` covers the 3D movement, camera, and combat simulation. **Every number that shapes the 3D jump — the spring's wind and reach, the arc's gravity, the run it is measured against, the hover, the glide, the dive, the pads and the wall kick — is written down once in `src/game3d/jumptuning.ts`**, which the controller spreads into `MOVE`; the file's header explains what each one does and tabulates what the current set produces, so the feel can be retuned there without reading the simulation.

Run `pnpm test`, `pnpm typecheck`, `pnpm lint`, and `pnpm build`. The actual-engine traversal audit tests every one of the 405 required route transitions, including real head/side collisions. It also checks inversion recovery and laser targeting for every enemy/boss. Separate controller/audio/combat tests cover input quarantine, connection changes, right-stick deadzone, looping/track routing, armor, stomps, boss phases, rear ambushes, parry staggers and reflected shots. The camera/look/parry/shield/ledge-catch/wall-kick, level-design and animation reports are under `qa/`.

Visual QA includes browser checks and 100 offscreen scenes made with the production renderer. Automated numerical and visual checks do not substitute for a measured full-campaign playthrough or physical Xbox latency testing; completion times are intentionally not claimed as measured.

The browser exposes three small WebMCP tools when supported: read game status, pause, and open instructions. Localhost additionally exposes bounded development scene/step tools; these are absent on the deployed origin. All state and assets are local to the browser; no game-account backend is required.
