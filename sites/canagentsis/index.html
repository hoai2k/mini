The story, as the band tells it

In the autumn of 1887 five musicians from the north shore of Cape Breton took passage on a herring boat out of Sydney, meaning to play the winter at the lumber camps up the St. Lawrence. The boat was the St. Patrick No. 17. The fog came down off Anticosti, stayed with them through the lakes, and lifted, by their account, somewhere off the Toronto Islands with a very tall tower standing where no tower had any business to be.

They have been in Toronto harbour ever since. The band does not explain this and does not much care to. They keep the boat at a slip on the east side of the harbour, play on deck when the weather allows and in the nearest tavern when it does not, and hold that a strathspey played properly sounds the same in any century.

The music is the old Cape Breton repertoire: marches into strathspeys into reels, the fiddle leading, guitar under it like a keel, a whistle for the airs and a bodhrán to keep the deck moving. They sing in Gaelic when the song calls for it and in English when it does not, and they take requests, provided the request was written before 1900.

The crew

Five hands, as photographed on deck.

  • Angus MacNeil

    Fiddle · leader

    Of Iona, Cape Breton. Learned the strathspeys from his grandmother and has never once played one at the written tempo. Calls the sets, names the tunes, and settles arguments about both.

  • Iain Beaton

    Tin whistle · flute · Gaelic song

    Of Mabou. Carries the airs and the slow songs, and the only member who admits to having looked at the tower. He says it is well made.

  • Duncan Gillis

    Guitar · piano when there is one

    Of Judique. The keel of the band; his chords under a reel are the reason the deck does not tip. Keeps the ship's log, which is mostly set lists.

  • Rory MacIsaac

    Bodhrán · step dance

    Of Ingonish. The only one who stands, because a bodhrán wants the whole deck. Step-dances on the hatch cover when the reels come round, which they always do.

  • Màiri Campbell

    Fiddle · voice

    Of Baddeck. Second fiddle by seating and first by reputation. Sings the milling songs and the laments, and wrote Lake Ontario, Wide and Deep, the only new song the band allows itself.

The song

The band has recorded one thing, Lake Ontario, Wide and Deep. They consider that plenty.

I

Lake Ontario, Wide and Deep

Song · fiddles, whistle, guitar and bodhrán · 3 min 19 s

Màiri's song, and the only new one the band allows itself. Written at the east slip, facing the water, for a lake that is not the sea and a city that was not there when they set out. Recorded on deck in a single take, the harbour audible under it.

Canagentsis is an imaginary band. The photograph and the story are made for the project; the song is real, and theirs. Cape Breton's real fiddle tradition is very much alive, and nothing here is meant to stand in for it.
